Interfacial phenomena, as diverse as molecular transport, mechanical failure, adhesion, wetting, and stimuli-responsiveness, govern the performance and reliability of a remarkably broad class of functional materials. They are controlled not solely by device-scale structures but also by molecular-scale interactions.

My research mission is to develop interactive and innovative polymer designs that enable the rational engineering of interfacial phenomena through molecularly encoded functionality. This goal demands the integration of three complementary dimensions. At the design level, I investigate how specific molecular interactions, including polarity, ionic structures, and steric effects, can be encoded into monomer and polymer architecture to program interfacial function. At the synthetic level, the development of synthetic routes for desired monomers and their controlled polymerization methods provides the tools to translate those design intentions into well-defined structures. The structural analyses reveal the relationship between materials architecture across length scales and macroscopic interfacial properties. The critical insight driving this framework is that these three dimensions are not independent; the full potential of each is realized only when they are considered in concert.

Self-healing polymers

Materials capable of self-repair can significantly improve their lifetime. Self-healing in polymers is governed by interchain interactions and chain mobility, both of which can be tailored by glass transition temperature (Tg) of copolymers. Monomers whose homopolymer has high Tg provide elastic energy, whereas monomers whose homopolymer has low Tg provide segmental mobility. Random copolymers with an optimized composition of these monomers exhibit self-healing at a desired temperature, and a larger Tg mismatch between these monomers can accelerate the healing process.
